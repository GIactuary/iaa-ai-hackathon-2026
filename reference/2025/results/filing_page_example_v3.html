Section 4 · Model development

Model type, target, data splits, variable selection and tuning

What the reviewer asks

Describe the model type and why it suits its role, how data was split into training and validation sets, the target variable, the link and distribution and why, and how variables were selected.

NAIC B.1.a, B.1.c, B.1.f, B.1.g, B.2.d

The target is log of sale price. Fitting in logs is the same choice as a log-link model: effects multiply, and errors are measured in percentages. Eight models from three families were fitted, and a weighted blend of their predictions is the filed model.

Exhibit 4.1

Each house is held out once and predicted by a model fitted on the other four fifths. Every score in this filing uses those out-of-fold predictions.

Non-negative, summing to one, fitted by least squares on out-of-fold predictions. Ridge, Elastic Net and XGBoost add nothing that Lasso and LightGBM do not already supply.
How to read this
  • Cross-validation is a back-test run five times. Like testing a reserving method on accident years it was not fitted to, each house is predicted by a model that never saw it.
  • Exhibit 4.3: each dot is one fold split. The spread along a row, typically 0.002 to 0.004, is luck of the split. Two models closer than that cannot be told apart on one split.
  • Lasso penalty (alpha) is credibility in another form. A larger alpha pulls more coefficients to exactly zero. At the chosen alpha the model uses 121 of 339 inputs; at the left edge it uses 266 and fits noise.
  • Learning curve: after 4,000 trees the training error is 0.041 but the held-out error is 0.098. The model looks more than twice as good on houses it has seen. That gap is overfitting, and it is why only held-out scores count.
  • Blend weight of 37% for Lasso means the final log price is 37% Lasso's view, 35% LightGBM's, and so on. In dollars it is a weighted geometric average.

How to read this
  • 95% CI of gain (Exhibit 5.1): I resampled the houses 2,000 times. In 95% of resamples the proposed model still won, by between the two figures shown. Neither interval reaches zero.
  • Double lift sorts houses by the ratio of the two predictions. The left end holds houses where the proposed model is lowest relative to the current one. A line at 1.00 means that model's predictions equal actual prices on average. At the right end the current model's line sits at 1.06: where it disagrees most, it under-prices by about 6%, while the proposed model is within 0.1%.
  • Overfitting means a model has memorised its training houses rather than learned what drives price. Think of a student who memorised last year's exam: perfect on that paper, ordinary on a new one. Exhibit 5.4 shows exactly that gap. On the houses they were fitted to, both models look twice as good (0.04 to 0.05) as on houses they have not seen (about 0.09). That is why no number in this filing is measured on fitted houses.
  • Locked hold-out is a sealed envelope: 293 houses put aside at the start and opened once, after every choice was final. It is the closest thing here to the real test.
  • Coverage: of 100 houses, about 90 sold inside their 90% range overall. In the cheapest fifth only 81 did. The range is honest on average and too narrow at the ends, the same pattern as a reserve range that is fine in aggregate but thin for small and very large claims.
J5

Decide the shape of the 90% range. A flat ±13% under-covers both ends; for an insured-value use the low side matters most, because an under-valued home is underinsured.

Section 6 · The rating plan and how it relates to the model

The blend, read as base rate × factors

What the reviewer asks

Explain how the model produces rates. Compare model relativities with indicated one-way relativities, and explain adjustments. Advanced models need tools that make them interpretable.

NAIC C.1, C.3 · FSRA guidance, explainability (Appendix A)

The blend is a black box: eight models and a weighted average. To file it as a rating plan, I fitted a glass-box copy using MID (the method the 2025 winner wrote). MID finds the additive model, base plus one term per variable plus selected interactions, that best reproduces the blend's predictions. In log terms the terms add; in dollars they multiply. That is the structure of a log-link GLM rating plan.

Exhibit 6.1

Overall quality relativities from the glass box

Exhibit 6.2

How faithful the rating plan is to the model

Fidelity on test rows is the winner's measure: how much of the black box's variation the plan fails to reproduce. Out-of-sample error is my addition: the plan fitted on training folds and scored on held-out houses against actual prices. My black-box figure here uses weights fitted on all folds, so it reads about 0.0005 better than the honest 0.0893.
How to read this
  • Relativity 1.09 for grade 8: a grade-8 house is priced 9% above an otherwise identical house of average quality. Grade 10 is 19% above, the lowest band 15% below.
  • Unexplained variation 0.13%: across the test houses, the rating plan reproduces 99.87% of the variation in the black box's prices. It is close to a perfect copy on the houses it was fitted to.
  • Out-of-sample cost +6.1%: on new houses the copy is less accurate than the original, 0.0942 against 0.0887. The plan explains the model well but does not fully replace it. Section 10, J4, records the choice.
J4

Decide what is filed: the blend, explained by the rating plan, or the rating plan itself. The first is more accurate; the second is what a consumer or regulator can follow line by line.

Section 9 · Segment and fairness testing

Is any group consistently over- or under-priced?

What the reviewer asks

Run loss ratio and control-variable tests, including multivariable effects. Test outputs for disparate impact and proxy effects.

FSRA guidance, outcomes testing

The housing data holds no protected characteristics, so a direct disparate-impact test is not possible. The nearest equivalent is calibration by segment: actual ÷ predicted (A/E) for each neighbourhood and quality grade. A model that is fair in this sense has A/E near 1.00 in every group with enough houses to judge.

Exhibit 9.1

A/E by quality grade

ProposedCurrent
Bars start at 1.00. Grades 2 and 10 drift furthest, and they hold 5 and 12 houses.
Exhibit 9.2

A/E by neighbourhood, 12 largest

How to read this
  • A/E of 1.00: actual prices equal predictions on average for that group. 1.07: actual prices ran 7% above prediction, so the group is under-priced. 0.86: over-priced by about 14%.
  • Small groups swing by chance. Five houses at A/E 0.86 is weak evidence; 215 houses at 1.00 is strong. Read A/E together with the count, as you would credibility-weight a territory's experience.
J7

Decide how to treat thin segments. Grade 10 (12 houses) is under-priced by about 7% in the proposed model and 10% in the current one. Options: accept, cap the relativity, or credibility-weight toward the one-way.
