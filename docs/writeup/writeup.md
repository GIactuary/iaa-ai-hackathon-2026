# A technical price built with AI and decided by an actuary

**IAA AI-Assisted Hackathon 2026, Parts 1 and 2.** Bavan Amirthalingam, FCAS, FCIA, appointed actuary for the entry. Code, evidence files, decision briefs and the AI log: [repository link]. Every number below comes from a committed evidence file in `outputs/` or a decision note in `decisions/`; Part 2's exhibits are generated by `scripts/exhibits_part2.py`.

## Summary

We priced 2025 motor, home buildings and home contents twice: for an insurer in a developed market with four years of data (Part 1), then for an emerging market with two years and a 2025 book that is 76% new business from an acquired broker portfolio (Part 2). One rule decided every modelling choice in both parts: a change stays only if it lowers deviance on a later year out of time and in cross-validation, with a 95% customer-bootstrap interval above zero. Each rule and test design was committed to version control before the test ran.

**Part 1** finished 15th on the private leaderboard at 0.13641 (first 0.13923; places 5 to 20 lie within 0.0019). A blend of model families per line, set to a weather-normalised 2025 level, beat our working GLM on customers locked away until the end. **Part 2** borrows Part 1's risk shape, fitted on a million developed-market rows, and lets the emerging data set the level and add only what it proves: credibility applied to a whole rating plan. On customers locked away until the end, the borrowed shape held in motor and contents and failed in buildings, so buildings went back to the plain Part 1 GLM. Four independent AI reviews then found that our own line-by-line rule, applied consistently, also favoured pricing motor by peril; that change and a corrected credibility standard for new business make the final file. [Public score of the final file, from the actuary's upload.]

The appointed actuary decided every judgment, on decision briefs that set out options, evidence and a recommendation; 48 exchanges are logged, including those where the AI was wrong; in Part 1 he proposed the level method himself, and in Part 2 he chose against the AI on the credibility standard.

## 1. Part 1: a technical price for an established book

**Use and data.** The price is a technical risk premium, the expected ultimate cost of each 2025 row, to set rates, measure adequacy against the current premium and refer risks the evidence cannot support (J1). Buildings ran at a loss ratio of 1.29 to 1.36 in every year from 2021 to 2024. The data is synthetic: 2.4 million policy rows, 163,150 claims valued at 31 December 2024, 626,639 rows to price. The target had to be built: net cost by loss date, repudiated claims out, claims capped (100k motor, 200k buildings, 30k contents), and each row's exposure scaled by its expected reporting completion, which acts as IBNR at row level. Nine stated rules repair the planted errors on training and test rows alike (sum insured entered 1,000 times too large on 2,797 training and 730 test rows, for example). No record was excluded; an 11.9M bodily injury reserve was investigated and kept as a genuine large loss (X1).

**Validation.** Every choice was judged as a 2025 price is set: fit 2021 to 2022 and score 2023 (A), fit 2021 to 2023 and score 2024 (B). The metric is the competition's Tweedie deviance at power 1.5, judged after each line is rescaled to its actual total, as rating plans are compared after an off-balance; the level is a separate judgment. A random 15% of customers (73,227) was locked away before any fit and opened once, after the freeze.

**Exhibit 1. Each kept step, out of time, all lines**

| Step (register item) | Deviance saved on 2023 [95%], on 2024 | Ranking D², 2023 / 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Current premium as the price | | 8.01% / 7.28% |
| Plain Tweedie GLM, completion offset | Beats the current premium in every line | 9.98% / 9.42% |
| Claims history with credibility (L1 to L3) | +114,842 [68,647, 164,656], +143,043 | 10.34% / 9.95% |
| Three features in, five factors out: GLM "W2" | Motor +79,907 [45,218, 112,951], +61,068 | 10.51% / 10.12% |
| Blend of model families (O1, O2) | +92,357 [46,571, 139,010], +114,384 | 10.67% / 10.33% |

*Sources: `outputs/phase4_baselines.json`, `outputs/phase5_*.json`. The cap and excess-factor step (+32,825 on 2023) sits between rows 2 and 3.*

> **How to read this.** Ranking D² is the share of a flat rate's deviance the model removes. The blend's +92,357 is 0.18% of the W2 GLM's 2023 deviance: small, because most deviance is the chance of who claims, but its interval sits above zero and it won in all 2,000 customer resamples.

More failed than passed: of 67 engineered features three stayed, and climate normals and plain gradient boosting (−242,328 on 2023) lost to the GLM, because the data behaves like a multiplicative rating plan. **Claims history** is experience rating with credibility: each policy's mod is (A + k) ÷ (E + k), with A its prior claims and E its class's expected claims; in motor k = 2.76, so a policy expecting 0.37 claims over three years carries 1.17 after one claim and 0.90 claim-free. The **families** (GLM, an explainable additive model, boosted corrections to the GLM, CatBoost) were tuned on inner folds and weighted per line on one scoring, tested on the other.

**Level.** The 2025 indication is built peril by peril from weather-normalised experience, the method the appointed actuary proposed when the AI's own recommendation turned out to rest on a bug (K4, D16): frequency restated to normal weather, trends fitted on the restated years within −5% to +12% a year, then the realised 2025 weather. A motor row the GLM rates at 500 CU at the 2024 level is priced at 500 × 1.058 (cost above the cap) × 1.009 (to the 2025 indication) × 0.9996 (2025 weather) = 533.5 CU. Expected 2025 cost over current premium: motor 0.95, buildings 1.43, contents 0.83.

> **How to read this.** The blend saves deviance in every line and year; across lines the 2023 interval is above zero, so it passes the keep rule on unseen customers. One 11.9M claim is 43.8% of the hold-out's 2023 motor cost, which is why motor's interval is wide.

**Referral and result.** Rows beyond the evidence (sum insured over 1.25 times its segment's training maximum, segments under 100 rows, families disagreeing by over 30%) are listed for underwriting review; only in motor did a changed price pass the keep rule (1,516 rows take the GLM's price). The final file refits the frozen pipeline on every customer. It scored 0.12908 public (5th at the time) and **0.13641 private, 15th; the winner scored 0.13923**. Limitations: one claim moves the motor level by about 3%, and realised 2025 weather is used, which a real 2025 price would not have (at most 0.3% by line).

## 2. Part 2: the same pipeline in an emerging market

**What changed.** Two years (2023 and 2024), six regions, ages in bands, no credit score, mileage or telematics, occupation typed by the customer, local construction codes, and gender, which this market allows in pricing. The training book is 86% renewals; the 2025 book is 76% new business. Part 1's code ran unchanged with a market configuration; its data rules stopped on the new construction codes until a map was written, and two rules were added: open claims net of expected recoveries (R10) and occupation cleaned (R11).

**Borrow the shape, learn the level (P2-C (b)).** Before any model we tested whether two emerging years could carry their own risk shape. Fitted on 2023 and scored on 2024, Part 1's relativities beat a GLM fitted on emerging data in every line (buildings D² 11.5% against −144%: a few large claims make a one-year emerging GLM unusable). The developed market is the complement; the emerging data earns weight only where it shows it.

**The test (P2-V), written before the first candidate.** A change is kept if it lowers deviance out of time (fit 2023, score 2024) and in customer-grouped cross-validation on three seeds, with the cross-validation interval from 2,000 customer resamples above zero. 15% of customers were locked away first. Rounds 1 to 3 judged that interval pooled over the three lines; from the hold-out on, the actuary judged it line by line. The test has limited power: it can confirm a gain of about 0.002 of D² in motor and 0.004 in buildings, so "failed" often means "not proven".

**Exhibit 3. What passed (full ladder and every candidate: Appendix D)**

| Step | Out of time | Cross-validation [95%] | D², cross-validation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Check file: Part 1 shape at the emerging level (K0) | | | 0.1402 |
| + gender in motor, fire and flood with address hazards, Part 1's tree in contents, each row's own-year weather | +7,498 | +37,526 [298, 76,858] | 0.1442 |

*Sources: `outputs/markets/part2/part2_candidates_round2.json`, `part2_tuning.json`, `part2_motor_peril.json`; the premium step in `decisions/part2_m2_design.md`. The last row is on every customer, so its D² is on a different base.*

> **How to read this.** Positive is deviance saved against the row above. The combination saves 37,526 in cross-validation, about 0.004 of D², and its interval only just clears zero. Ten other candidates failed, among them a credibility re-rate of every factor, a per-factor credibility re-rate in motor, settings tuned on Part 1 rows and a boosted correction learned on emerging rows. The boosted correction stopped at 1 to 57 trees: nothing large enough for our test to confirm, which rules out a single missed effect worth about 0.005 of D² but not smaller ones. Prior-year claims history was tested in the customer-across-lines form Part 1 had rejected; in Part 1's own form it gains in motor in both tests (+4,750; +4,362 [−3,130, 14,186]) but does not pass.

**The hold-out and a line-by-line call.** It was opened once, on a rule committed before opening: if the frozen model beat the check file's shape in neither reading, the actuary decides.

**Exhibit 4. Frozen model against the check file's shape, deviance saved**

| Reading | Motor | Buildings | Contents | Pooled [95%] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hold-out 2024 rows, fitted on 2023 | +1,971 | −769 | −2,189 | −987 [−9,648, 7,430] |
| Every hold-out row | +4,789 | −17,484 | +1,048 | −11,648 [−46,177, 11,635] |

*Sources: `outputs/markets/part2/part2_holdout.json`; register item H-F.*

> **How to read this.** Positive means the frozen model is better. Pooled, it fell short within noise; by line, motor gains in every reading and contents in three of four, while buildings loses in three of four. Buildings is a line of twelve events: twelve total-loss fires make up 28% of emerging buildings cost, and every buildings reading rests on where they land.

The actuary kept motor and contents and took buildings back to the Part 1 GLM (H-F (b)), applying the keep rule line by line. Four independent AI reviews of the final file, in separate sessions, then found the inconsistency: round 1 had rejected motor priced by peril on the pooled interval (motor +7,348 and +8,285; pooled +249) and the line rule was never applied back. Rebuilt in our own pipeline with every reviewed figure reproduced, it passes in motor and gains on the hold-out customers (+3,454 [448, 7,075] on their 2024 rows; +5,952 [253, 13,884] on every row). The emerging motor mix differs from Part 1's (third-party property 34.4% of capped cost against 29.9%, own damage 18.9% against 22.1%), and a whole-line GLM carries Part 1's mix inside every relativity. Third-party-only covers rise about 8%, comprehensive falls about 2%. Both decisions came after the hold-out was opened and are reported as such (H-F, H-MP).

**The level and new business.** As in Part 1: a 2025 indication per peril at normal weather with Part 1's trends (two years cannot fit one), excess factors (motor 1.007 from its own large claims, property from Part 1's shares), then each row's 2025 weather. New business, 76% of the book, takes a credibility-weighted factor: new against renewal, actual over model, moved toward the emerging figure by Z = √(n ÷ (1,082 × (1 + CV²))), the limited-fluctuation standard for a pure premium. Motor: 697 claims, severity CV² 4.15, Z 0.35, factor 1.050. The first file used the claim-count standard (Z 0.80, factor 1.114); two reviews found the mismatch, and the actuary chose the pure-premium standard although our tests cannot separate the two (H-NS (b), against the AI's recommendation to keep and disclose). Expected 2025 cost over written premium: motor 0.92, buildings 1.23, contents 0.74. Ten rows are referred for underwriting review at the model price (H-R (a)).

**From risk premium to office premium (Appendix A).** For each of the 20 reference policies, annual basis: office premium = (risk premium + catastrophe load at 12% of property risk premium + 85 CU) ÷ (1 − 15% variable − commission (broker 20%, direct 0%) − 8% premium tax − 8% profit) (OP-T (a), OP-B (a)). Buildings policy B00229821, broker: 370.1 + 44.4 + 85 = 499.6 CU, ÷ 0.49 = 1,019.5 CU, against 285.6 CU charged today. The expense table drives much of this: a broker policy needs about twice its risk premium plus 173 CU. On risk premium alone, before any expense, all seven buildings reference policies already cost more than they are charged. The acquired broker book is priced well below its technical premium. No reference policy is sold through a comparison site, whose commission the table does not give (P2-E (d)).

**Gender (Appendix B).** It enters motor only, as one factor fitted on emerging rows on top of a shape that has none (Part 1's data carries no gender). Without it, men's actual cost runs 1.136 times what the Part 1 shape expects and women's 0.865. With it, a man pays 1.30 times what an otherwise identical woman pays (95% interval 1.15 to 1.48); removing gender at the same motor total would lower men's prices by 11.6% and raise women's by 15.3%. Appendix B shows each reference driver's price both ways.

**Limitations.** The public leader is 0.019 of D² ahead of our earlier file. At the board's size two equally good but different models differ by a standard deviation of about 0.004, so most of that gap is real, and our tests could not find where it comes from; the level is not the cause, since one line off by 10% costs under 0.001. Buildings rests on twelve fires and Part 1's shape and excess load. The new-business factor is the largest judgment in the 2025 level and rests on 77 buildings and 102 contents claims. Realised 2025 weather is used. [K17 and K18, two structural tests run after the final file: result to be added.]

## 3. Use of AI, and the guardrails around it

AI worked in roles set by standing prompts (data steward, modeller, filing writer, independent reviewer; `ai/prompt_library.md`). Each decision went to the appointed actuary as a brief with options, evidence and a recommendation; he decided, challenged or proposed his own method. Every exchange that changed a decision is a card in `ai/ai_log.json` (48 cards: question, AI answer, challenge, test, result, decision).

**Exhibit 5. Where the AI was wrong or was challenged**

| Exchange | What happened | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| D16, Part 1 level | Asked why before choosing, the AI found a bug in its own completion weights; the actuary proposed weather normalisation | 2024 motor level error from 2.0% to 0.3% |
| D17, D18, D22, D23, D28 | AI errors caught by checks: a credibility estimator that was mostly noise; thin blank levels priced at 0.6 CU a year (reached the first baseline file); a penalty reused on the wrong scale; "every cost is positive" checked without the hold-out | Each fixed before the final file |
| D31, Part 1 post-mortem | The AI ranked missing weather as the likely cause of the gap to the top; it was wrong | Weather failed (−5,623 across lines); the premium term found instead |
| D35 to D37, code threshold | The AI recommended 20 against its own pre-registered rule; Part 1 evidence then contradicted its stated reason | The actuary set 11, on both markets' evidence |
| D36, run time | The AI blamed the model search for a run of several days; the actuary's question led to a profile | A quadratic fold split; 36 minutes instead of days |
| D42, public board | The actuary proposed tuning on the public board; the AI measured its noise first and pushed back | A written rule for board use (M2-R) |
| D47, D48, gap reviews | Four independent reviews found our own rule applied inconsistently and a credibility standard misapplied; one review misread the board's noise and another corrected it | Motor by peril and the pure-premium standard adopted |

> **How to read this.** Each row is a logged card. In D36 the AI recommended stopping a threshold test as too slow; asked whether more machines would help, it profiled the run and found one thread doing 12.7 of 13.8 CPU-hours in a customer-grouped fold split, quadratic on text ids. The fix finished the run in 36 minutes, and its results overturned the reason recorded for the threshold.

**Guardrails.** Every test design is committed before the test runs, and every threshold is dated before use (`decisions/thresholds*.md`; the code refuses undated ones). Hold-outs were opened once, on written rules. The public board was used for one paired check decided before upload. Exclusions need a written reason (none were made). Gender is shown with its effect and the price without it. Both final files rebuild from the raw data with one command each and match the uploaded files byte for byte (`scripts/reproduce.py`).
