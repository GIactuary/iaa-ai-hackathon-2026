How much should the emerging price borrow from Part 1's experience?

Why it matters

With about 1,000 property claims and 7,000 motor claims, relativities fitted on emerging data alone are noisy. Part 1 has 16 to 25 times as much experience on the same fields. This is the adaptation story the judges asked about, and it decides how every family is built.

What the data shows

I fitted three tree models and scored them on emerging rows they had not seen. E uses emerging data only. D is fitted on Part 1 data only and applied to emerging rows. DE starts from D's prediction and lets the emerging data move it where it has evidence. Every prediction is rescaled to the actual level, so this tests the risk shape (who costs more than whom), not the level.

How to read this. The figures in the first table are D², the share of deviance explained against a flat rate, the same form as the competition metric. On claim count out of time, the emerging-only model explains 4.5% and the Part 1 model 6.2%. In the second table that gap is 357 deviance units, with an interval of 267 to 446, well clear of zero. D beats E in every line, target and test. Letting the emerging data adjust D (DE) adds nothing on frequency and loses a little on cost.

In pricing terms: Part 1's relativities rank emerging risks better than relativities fitted on emerging's own two years. The emerging data's job is the level, plus whatever is genuinely different (region, gender, the motor large-loss tail on P2-L), each tested on its own.

Limits: default tree settings and no tuning. Region, make_model, occupation and gender were left out of D (the regions differ between markets, and the two codes need encoding). This is exploratory evidence for this decision, not a model choice.

(a) Emerging data only

Every family is fitted on 2023-2024 emerging rows, as the pipeline stands. Simplest to explain. It gives up the gain in the table: about a quarter of the explained deviance on frequency, and more than half on cost.

(b) Part 1 shape, emerging level and additionsRecommended

Each family's base is fitted on Part 1 rows on the fields both markets share (ages mapped to the emerging bands). The emerging data sets the level through the level chain. Emerging-only terms (region, gender if P2-S allows it, the encoded codes) enter on top, each kept only if it passes the test in P2-V. The family weights are fitted on emerging rows. The write-up explains it as a credibility complement, the way you would price a new territory from the countrywide plan.

(c) Pool both markets in one fit, with a market effect

One model on both markets' rows, with a market indicator. The data decides how far the markets differ, factor by factor in the GLM and by splits in the trees. Not tested yet. It needs about half a day more to build and test.

Recommendation: (b).The test shows Part 1's shape beats emerging's own in every line and in both tests. Letting the emerging data rebuild the shape loses accuracy. (b) uses the larger experience for what it measures well and the emerging data for what only it can measure.
P2-VOne-way door: every test uses it

How is every choice tested, with only two years of data?

Why it matters

Every keep-or-drop call in the build goes through this test: features, families, weights, gender, the borrowing in P2-C. Part 1's keep rule needs two out-of-time scorings (fit 2021-2022 and score 2023; fit 2021-2023 and score 2024). The emerging market allows only one: fit 2023 and score 2024.

What the data shows
  • The 2024 scoring year holds 3,491 motor, 537 buildings and 507 contents claims.
  • In the Y2-T test, out-of-time scoring alone was noisy. Threshold 6 looked 75 deviance units worse than the best out of time, but tied in cross-validation. Refitting one candidate with a different seed moved its total by about 100.
  • Cross-validation uses both years in every fit, so it has twice the data. But it never scores a later year than it fits, so it cannot catch year-to-year drift.
  • A credibility-encoded code needs two periods per level. With only 2023 fitted, the out-of-time test uses calendar quarters as periods, as Y2-T did.
(a) Both tests, the interval on cross-validationRecommended

Out of time (fit 2023, score 2024 with reporting completion) and customer-grouped five-fold cross-validation over 2023-2024 with seeds 11, 22 and 33. A choice is kept only if it lowers deviance in both, and its 95% paired bootstrap interval on cross-validation lies above zero. This is Part 1's rule with the second scoring replaced.

(b) Out of time only

Mirrors pricing a later year exactly. On one year of claims many real gains will not clear the interval, so the model stays thinner than the data supports.

(c) Cross-validation only

The most data per test. It never checks the model against a year it has not seen.

Recommendation: (a).It keeps Part 1's structure (two tests, one with a confidence condition) and puts the confidence condition on the test with twice the data. A choice that only works when the years are mixed fails the out-of-time leg.
P2-HOne-way door: once drawn and used

Do we lock away a hold-out of emerging customers for one final check?

Why it matters

Part 1 locked 15% of customers and opened them once, before the final file. That untouched check carries the trust and validation criterion (20% of judging). Here it costs data in a market that is already thin.

What the data shows

A 15% hold-out would hold about 520 motor, 80 buildings and 76 contents claims in 2024, and about twice that over both years. That is enough for a level check and a motor D² with a usable interval. Property intervals would be wide.

(a) 15% of customers (seed 2026), opened once, then refit on everythingRecommended

Locked before any model is fitted, as in Part 1. Every build test runs on the other 85%. After the hold-out is opened once, the final models are refitted on all rows, so the submitted file uses all the data.

(b) No hold-out

The build uses all rows. The final check is the out-of-time and cross-validation results and the public leaderboard. The public board uses only a third of test rows, and choosing by it would mean tuning to the board.

(c) 10% of customers

Less data held back, and a weaker final check: about 350 motor and 50 to 55 property claims in 2024.

Recommendation: (a).The untouched check is what makes the validation claims believable. Refitting on all rows after the one opening means the price itself is built on all the data.

Which factors go into the level GLM (W2) in the emerging market?

Why it matters

The W2 GLM sets the level chain, and the tree families start from it. It cannot run now: it uses four Part 1 fields this market does not have.

What the data shows
  • Missing here: exact driver and policyholder age (this market gives five bands: 17-24, 25-34, 35-49, 50-64, 65+), credit band, annual mileage and telematics.
  • Everything else W2 uses is present. Region has 6 levels (E01 to E06) instead of 12.
  • Claims history: it exists only for 2024 rows of 2023 customers. The test book is 76% new business, so most 2025 rows have none. Declared prior claims are on every customer.
(a) Part 1's W2, adapted, then a drop-one testRecommended

Age as the five given bands. Credit, mileage and telematics removed. Policy history and declared history kept, and the step 5 additions kept (motor voluntary excess and vehicle age; buildings trees within 5 m with clay soil). Each factor then faces a drop-one test on emerging data under P2-V, and failures are dropped. Under P2-C (b), the relativities come from the Part 1 fit and the emerging data sets the level.

(b) Part 1's W2, adapted, without the drop-one test

Faster. Carries Part 1's choices into this market untested.

(c) Rebuild the factor list from scratch on emerging data

The full step 5 selection, on two years of data. The most work, and the selection itself would be noisy at this data size.

Recommendation: (a).It starts from a factor list that earned its place on 20 times the data, and still checks each factor against this market.

Large-loss caps and the excess factor that puts the capped cost back

Why it matters

The models are fitted on cost capped per claim (Part 1: motor 100,000, buildings 200,000, contents 30,000). The excess above the cap returns through an excess-loss factor (ELF) on the level. The ELF moves each line's level directly: an ELF of 1.05 against 1.01 is 4% on every motor price.

How to read this. Emerging has 450 motor bodily injury claims. At Part 1's rate of claims above 100,000 we would expect 9.4 of them above the cap; there are 3. A count that low has a 2% chance if the tails were alike, so emerging's bodily injury tail is lighter. That fits its motor claims costing about half Part 1's on average. Fire (6 against 4.0) and flood (4 against 4.9) are consistent with Part 1, and 6 or 4 claims are too few to set a tail on their own.

The caps themselves matter less, because the ELF returns whatever they cut. Emerging's 99.9th-percentile claim is 59,818 in motor, 337,871 in buildings and 44,253 in contents, so the Part 1 caps cut only the largest few claims.

(a) Part 1 caps; mixed ELFRecommended

Motor uses its own tail (ELF 1.007), because emerging's bodily injury tail is shown to be lighter. Buildings and contents use Part 1's excess shares by peril (1.038 and 1.023), because emerging's few large claims agree with them and cannot set a tail alone.

(b) Part 1 caps; emerging's own ELF everywhere

Buildings goes to 1.066, set by 6 fire claims. That is 2.7% higher on every buildings price than (a).

(c) Part 1 caps; Part 1's excess shares everywhere

Motor goes to 1.051, applying Part 1's heavier bodily injury tail. That is 4.3% higher on every motor price than (a), against the evidence.

Recommendation: (a).It uses each market for what it can show: the emerging data where it shows a real difference, Part 1 where emerging has too few large claims to say anything.

How are the blanks treated, now that there are five times as many?

What the data shows

About 11 fields are each blank on about 12% of rows, so 73% to 76% of training rows have at least one blank rating field. Against complete rows, the frequency ratio for rows with a blank is 0.95 [0.90, 1.00] in motor, 1.00 [0.88, 1.15] in buildings and 0.86 [0.75, 0.99] in contents. Field by field, the ratios sit near 1. The clearest exception is buildings days unoccupied (blank 0.76 [0.62, 0.94]). Part 1 also found blanks carried no signal.

(a) Part 1's treatmentRecommended

Recover a blank from the same policy's other rows where the rules allow. The rest become their own level in the GLM and are handled natively by the trees, so a blank with a signal (days unoccupied) gets its own estimate.

(b) Impute from other fields

A model per field. It adds steps to explain, and the blanks carry no signal to recover.

(c) Drop rows with blanks

Removes three-quarters of the data. Not viable.

Recommendation: (a).The blanks look random against claims, and their own level captures the one field where they are not.
