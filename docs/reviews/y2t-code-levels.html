Recommendation: 20 levels. The level matters at one boundary only. Let occupation in raw (any threshold of 80 or more) and prediction gets clearly worse in both tests. Anywhere from 4 to 20 the tests cannot tell the thresholds apart: the differences are smaller than the spread between repeated fits of the same model. 20 is the best threshold in cross-validation and within reach of the best out of time, and it encodes only the two codes where raw entry is shown to hurt.

How to read this

Each figure is extra claim-frequency deviance against the best threshold in that test, summed over motor, buildings and contents. Lower is better, and 0 is the best. The 95% interval resamples customers. An interval that includes zero means the threshold cannot be told apart from the best.

Example: in cross-validation, threshold 100 lets occupation in raw. It sits 321 above the best, with an interval of 240 to 401, far from zero. It is also 307 worse than not using occupation or make_model at all. Threshold 4 sits 21 above the best (−15 to +55), so it is indistinguishable from the best, yet it is 6 worse than not using the two codes at all.

Scale: the best total is about 39,870, so the gaps among thresholds 4 to 20 are 0.02% to 0.05% of it. Refitting the same threshold with another seed moves its total by about 100. Threshold 20 scored 39,831, 39,840 and 39,934.

The options

(a) 20 levels

Recommended
Does
Encodes make_model and occupation; every code with 8 levels or fewer enters raw. Any value from 8 to 79 gives the same model in the emerging market.
Evidence
Best in cross-validation; within reach of the best out of time (+12, −15 to +40). Ahead of 4 in every cross-validation seed (by 44, 16 and 1). Beats leaving the codes out by 14 in cross-validation and by 35 out of time.
Costs
None measured. Inside Part 1's evidence: occupation raw at 20 levels passed there.

(b) 4 levels, the rule written before the test

Does
Also encodes every five- to eight-level code: driver and policyholder age band, body type, soil, region, energy rating, colour.
Evidence
The pre-registered rule picks it in both tests, because it is the lowest threshold within reach of the best. It is still the weakest of the encoded thresholds in cross-validation, and below leaving the two codes out (−6).
Costs
The tree no longer sees age bands and regions as bands. Each becomes one smoothed number, which is harder to read in the write-up, and the tests show nothing gained in return.

(c) 7 levels

Does
Encodes vehicle colour as well as make_model and occupation.
Evidence
Best out of time; +8 in cross-validation. Indistinguishable from 20 in both. Colour carries almost no signal (levels about 1% apart in round 1), so encoding it changes little either way.

Thresholds of 80 or more are not offered: both tests reject them, with intervals far from zero.

Why I recommend against the rule I wrote

The rule assumed the lowest threshold is the most economical model, because it encodes the most codes. So when every threshold from 4 to 20 came out tied, it picked 4.

The results contradict that assumption. All the gain comes from encoding the two codes with many levels. Encoding the small codes as well buys nothing in either test, and it costs readability: an age band stops being a band in the tree. The point of diminishing returns runs the other way from what the rule assumed. Raising the threshold to 80 or more loses heavily, and lowering it below 20 gains nothing.

So 20 is the lowest threshold that does all the useful work. It encodes exactly the codes where raw entry is shown to hurt, and nothing else.

Two related findings:

  • Cleaning occupation (trimming spaces, ignoring case) makes no measurable difference once it is encoded. Check-in item 5 can be decided on readability alone.
  • The gain from the two codes is small. Cross-validation puts it at +14 (interval −14 to +40). The family weights and the keep rule will decide whether the wider trees are used at all.
