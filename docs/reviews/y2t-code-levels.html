How many levels may a code have and still enter the trees raw?

Round 1 1 decision open Two-way door: one number in the thresholds file Waiting on: your verdict below

Recommendation: 20 levels. In the emerging market, make_model and occupation are then entered as one credibility-weighted number each, and every other code enters raw. The deciding fact: in buildings and contents, occupation has 80 levels, 77 of them with fewer than 30 claims, and no measurable difference in claim frequency between them. As a raw category, the trees would have 80 chances to split on noise. Encoded, it carries a value of about zero.

Why this decision has come up

24 Oct · D32 · decided

You chose Y2 (a): the tree families get every legitimate field, not only the ones the GLM vetted, and the family weights decide how much the trees count. One condition came with it: a code with many levels enters only credibility-encoded, and the level count is written in the thresholds file before the run that uses it.

Part 1 post-mortem · the evidence behind the condition

Entered raw, make_model (300 levels) lost 414,287 of deviance in motor (PM2). With make_model removed, the same wide inputs passed across lines (PM2m), and occupation was in them raw at 20 levels.

25 Oct · D34 · built

Building it on the emerging market showed two codes far above anything Part 1 tested raw: make_model at 300 levels and occupation at 80, because occupation is free text here. The threshold decides which side of the line they fall on.

How to read this

Z is the weight a credibility estimate gives a level's own claim experience; the rest goes to the line average. "Spread the data supports" is how far apart the levels' true frequencies are, after the noise in their experience is taken out. Shaded rows are the codes the tree would actually see as typed.

Example: emerging motor occupation, cleaned, has 40 occupations with a median of 179 claims each. The data supports occupation frequencies about 14% apart, and a typical occupation's experience gets Z = 0.75, three quarters of the weight. That is a real rating signal, and the encoding keeps it. In buildings, the same field as typed has a median of 7 claims per occupation and no measurable spread at all (Z = 0), so the encoded value is zero for every occupation.

A code with few levels is safe raw even without signal: emerging vehicle colour has 8 levels of about 876 claims each, too large for a tree to find much noise in. The risk is many thin levels.

The options

(a) 20 levels

Recommended
Does
Emerging: make_model and occupation are encoded; every other code (8 levels or fewer) enters raw. Part 1: only make_model is encoded. Every value from 8 to 39 gives the same emerging split.
Costs
Nothing to build; it is the default the code already runs. Occupation enters the trees as one number. The trees can still combine it with age band or cover, but they cannot single out one occupation the encoding treats as average.
Breaks
Nothing found. Where occupation carries signal (motor), the encoding keeps about three quarters of it at the typical level. Where it carries none (property), the encoding carries none either.
Closes
Nothing. The keep rule and the family weights still decide whether the wider trees are used at all.

(b) 100 levels

Does
Only make_model is encoded. Occupation enters raw, at 80 levels as typed (40 if the cleaning rule is adopted).
Costs
In buildings and contents, 77 of the 80 raw occupations have fewer than 30 claims and the field shows no signal. LightGBM's own smoothing of categories is weaker than the credibility weighting, so the trees can fit splits there that will not hold in 2025.
Breaks
It goes beyond the Part 1 evidence: no code above 20 levels was ever tested raw. The one code that was, make_model at 300, lost 414,287.
Closes
Nothing. Its one advantage is in motor, where the trees could split on individual occupations without shrinkage, and Z = 0.51 to 0.75 says those levels are only partly credible.

(c) Another value

Does
Any value from 8 to 39 behaves exactly like (a) on the emerging data. Below 8, low-level codes such as vehicle colour and energy rating would be encoded as well, and energy rating in buildings is a strong raw signal (Z = 0.82, levels 29% apart). From 40 to 79, cleaned occupation would enter raw and the as-typed version would be encoded, so the result would hinge on the cleaning decision.
Costs
A choice between values that give the same result, or one that ties two decisions together.

Why I went with 20

When I proposed it, the reason was Part 1: let a code enter raw only as far as Part 1 saw a raw code work (occupation at 20 levels), and no further.

The emerging data now gives a second reason. The codes above the line are exactly the ones whose levels are thin. make_model has a median of 23 claims per level, and occupation in property 7 to 8. Below the line, every code has hundreds of claims per level. So 20 separates thin codes from thick ones in this market, not only in Part 1.

What would change my mind: evidence that a code above 20 levels has many levels that each carry a credible, distinct signal the encoding flattens. Motor occupation comes closest, at Z = 0.75 once cleaned, and the encoding keeps three quarters of that.

Related decisions, kept separate: occupation cleaning (check-in item 5) changes how many levels occupation has, but not which side of 20 it falls on. Gender is still out of the tree inputs until you rule on it.
