Evidence behind five settled calls

P1

Is there a claim-level development factor we can rely on?

For reporting delay, yes, by peril and at row level. For case-estimate development, no. The file is one snapshot with no history of case estimates, and the research confirms that claim-level IBNER methods need that history or closure dates. What one snapshot does support is the reporting-delay curve per peril, and it backtests well.

Peril2024 claims still to report, % of reportedBacktest: 2023 predicted at 31 Dec 2023 ÷ today
Motor bodily injury81.5%1.022
Buildings subsidence46.6%1.033
Contents escape of water8.1%1.003
Buildings escape of water7.3%0.998
Motor third-party property7.2%1.004
Ten other perils1.6% to 5.4%0.986 to 1.010
  • Late claims are not larger. Mean cost by report delay stays between 0.80 and 1.17 of the peril mean, so count completion stands in for amount completion.
  • Case estimates look unbiased in aggregate. For 11 of 15 perils the 2024 average incurred is within ±6% of the 2021 to 2023 trend.
  • Bodily injury dropped about 20% from 2023, and it shows in closed claims too (21.5k and 22.8k in 2021 and 2022, then 17.7k and 17.3k). It is a level change, not reserving. P9 deals with it.
  • The year factor alone leaves a bias. 2024 new-business motor rows have 34% of their expected bodily injury claims reported, against 59% for renewals, because they start later in the year. P10 deals with it.

How to read the backtest. We pretended it was 31 Dec 2023, used only claims reported by then, and predicted how many 2023 claims there would be. For bodily injury only 55% were reported at that date, yet the prediction came within 2.2% of today's count. A ratio of 1.000 is a perfect prediction.

P2

How is a base rate set in practice, and has anyone solved this problem before?

The plan follows the textbook split, and the closest public competition was won the same way.

  • Werner and Modlin, Basic Ratemaking: the base comes from the pure premium method, using developed and trended loss per exposure. The trend runs from the average accident date of the experience to the average accident date of the future period (ch. 6, 8). The off-balance step backs the base out once the relativities are set (ch. 14).
  • CAS Monograph 5: a year variable is "usually sufficient" to keep development and trend out of the relativities (§5.1.3). Out-of-time testing matters most for event perils such as wind (§4.3.1). Gini says nothing about the level (§7.2).
  • AIcrowd Insurance Pricing Game (2020 to 2021): 60,000 motor policies with four years of history, year five priced, scored out of time. The winner built claim-history features and a large-claim detector, and stacked seven models with a Tweedie GLM on top.
  • Calibration literature (Wüthrich and Merz; Fissler, Lorentzen and Mayer): a log-link Tweedie GLM and a boosted model do not reproduce totals on their own, and Tweedie deviance penalises a level error directly. This is the case for P11.
P3

What is a Tweedie model?

A pure premium GLM in one step. For a power between 1 and 2, the Tweedie distribution is a compound Poisson-gamma. The number of claims is Poisson and each claim is gamma, so a policy's total cost is exactly zero most of the time and a positive amount otherwise. The power p sets how the variance grows with the mean (variance = φ × meanp). At p = 1 it behaves like a frequency model, at p = 2 like a severity model, and the competition scores at p = 1.5, in between.

unit deviance at p = 1.5: d(y, μ) = 4 (√y − √μ)² / √μ D² = 1 − Σd(model) / Σd(constant rate)
Our price μPenalty if no claim (y = 0)Penalty if a 5,000 CU claim
100 CU401,474
50 CU282,291

How to read this. D² is the share of the constant-rate model's error that our prices remove, like R² for loss cost. Halving the price saves 12 points on a policy with no claim and costs 817 points on one with a claim, so the metric punishes under-pricing more, the same direction as our costlier error under J1. A price of zero on a policy that claims scores infinitely badly, so every prediction stays above zero.

One Tweedie model forces any factor that raises the mean to raise frequency and severity together (Quijano Xacur and Garrido, 2015). Frequency × severity by peril avoids that, which is why P3 tests both.

J-weather

Does weather drive loss cost, attritional or large?

Yes for four perils, and through claim counts only. We compared the same address across years, so location is held constant. Severity moves by no more than 5% anywhere.

PerilWeather measureFrequencyLarge-claim frequencySeveritySame measure, place to place
StormWind index1.271.040.961.84
SubsidenceDry summer1.632.101.042.78
Escape of waterFreezing days1.191.040.971.90
Motor glass and hailHail days1.661.971.041.94
FloodRainfall0.990.880.953.32

How to read this. Each figure is the highest fifth over the lowest fifth. The storm figure of 1.27 means that in an address's windiest years it had 27% more storm claims than in its calmest years. Large claims are those above the peril's 90th percentile. Flood is a place effect (3.3 times) with no year effect. In 2025 the wind index runs about 0.9 standard deviations above each address's own average, so the year adjustment raises storm. Freezing, hail and dry-summer readings run slightly below average and lower the other three perils a little. This matches the published work: Klawa and Ulbrich (2003) on storm losses above a local gust threshold, Scheel et al. (2013) on rainfall and water damage, and the ABI on dry-summer subsidence surges.

P7

Which families give the most accuracy for the least black box?

Boosting started from the GLM leads on accuracy, and the GLM keeps the tariff story. In published insurance studies, boosting beats a good GLM on claim frequency, and on severity the families tie.

StudyDataFinding
Wüthrich, CANN (2019)French motorGLM explains 5.1% of deviance; the GLM plus a learned correction explains 6.8%, a third more
Henckaerts et al. (NAAJ 2021)Belgian motorBoosting has the lowest frequency deviance on every fold; severity has no clear winner
Holvoet, Antonio, Henckaerts (2025)Four datasetsBoosting-anchored correction best on three of four for frequency; the GLM was enough on one
Krúpová, Rachdi, Guibert (2025)French motor severityBinned GLM, EBM, XGBoost and EBM with pairs all within 0.6%

The ranking for our pool:

  1. Boosting started from the GLM's prediction: the tree part reads as a bounded correction to a tariff.
  2. Plain boosting (LightGBM, CatBoost).
  3. EBM with pairwise terms: its shape functions are already rating tables.
  4. Spline GLM as the anchor and the filing baseline.

Regulators accept tree models with a dependence plot for every variable, importance with its method, the reasons for any interaction, and lift and double lift (NAIC CASTF tree-model appendix).

How a price is built · unchanged, with P10 and P11 marked

From base rate to the row's expected cost

price(row) = exposure × [ Σperil baseperil, 2025 × off-balance (P11) × GLM relativities × tree adjustment × weather-year factor ] + exposure × large-loss load
base 2025Developed and trended loss cost per unit of exposure, by line and peril (P1, P9).
GLM relativitiesThe rating plan, fitted with a year factor and the row completion offset (P10).
tree adjustmentThe strongest non-GLM family, as a bounded correction.
weather-year factorThe disclosed 2025 adjustment for storm, subsidence, escape of water and glass (J-weather).

Illustration with made-up numbers, not results: a half-year motor row, base 600 CU, relativities 1.30 × 0.85 = 1.105, tree adjustment 1.04, weather factor 1.00, load 18 CU a year. Price = 0.5 × (600 × 1.105 × 1.04 + 18) = 0.5 × (689.5 + 18) = 353.8 CU.
